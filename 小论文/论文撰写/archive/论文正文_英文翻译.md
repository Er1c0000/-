# Super-Resolution Periodic Jitter Decomposition via Cascaded Sparse Bayesian Learning and Least Squares Correction

## Abstract

Accurate decomposition of periodic jitter (PJ) from time interval error (TIE) sequences is essential for signal integrity assessment of high-speed serial links. Conventional FFT-based methods severely underestimate PJ amplitude due to spectral leakage under finite observation windows, while LASSO-based sparse recovery requires prior knowledge for regularisation tuning and introduces systematic amplitude shrinkage bias through its $L_1$ penalty. This paper proposes SBL-LS, a three-stage cascaded algorithm: first, FastRVM performs a coarse full-band scan to identify candidate frequency bands, compressing the effective dictionary size by approximately 62%; second, the Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) mechanism of Sparse Bayesian Learning achieves hyperparameter-free adaptive frequency locking; finally, unconstrained ordinary least squares recalculates amplitude coefficients on the identified support set, eliminating shrinkage bias when the support set is correctly identified. Experiments on a 2 Gbps synthetic link demonstrate that SBL-LS achieves PJ estimation errors within 0.24 ps across the 100 kHz–15 MHz band, substantially outperforming the 14.3–14.7 ps systematic error of LS-FFT. Moreover, SBL-LS is the only method among those compared that yields nonzero estimates for all four jitter components; however, the ISI component exhibits approximately 116% overestimation, attributable to residual collinearity between low-frequency PJ candidate columns and the ISI pattern indicator matrix. Improving the estimation accuracy of weak ISI components constitutes a key direction for future work. The proposed method is suited for offline analysis in high-end test instruments and automated test equipment (ATE) calibration scenarios.

**Keywords:** jitter decomposition, sparse Bayesian learning, least squares, signal integrity, high-speed serial link

## 1. Introduction

The rapid advancement of integrated circuit technology has propelled high-speed serial link data rates beyond the 32 Gbps threshold, imposing increasingly stringent constraints on timing accuracy and signal integrity. In such high-frequency communication environments, jitter—the dominant factor governing bit error rate (BER)—has become a critical challenge in high-speed link design [1, 2]. To accurately assess system performance and localise interference sources, capturing TIE sequences with equivalent-time sampling oscilloscopes and performing precise jitter decomposition is indispensable [3].

According to physical origins and statistical characteristics, total jitter (TJ) is conventionally decomposed into random jitter (RJ) and deterministic jitter (DJ), where DJ is further classified into inter-symbol interference (ISI), duty cycle distortion (DCD), and periodic jitter (PJ) [2]. The core objective of jitter decomposition is to separate and quantify each of these components from TIE sequences.

Several methodological paradigms have been established for jitter component decomposition, each with well-defined applicability boundaries. Among statistical-domain methods, the Dual-Dirac model approximates the total jitter probability density function as the convolution of two impulse functions with a Gaussian, enabling rapid RJ/DJ separation [4]; however, it inherently treats all deterministic jitter as an aggregate quantity and cannot resolve the frequency or amplitude of individual PJ tones. The tail-fit method further extracts RJ by fitting the extreme tails of the cumulative distribution function, yet its accuracy degrades substantially when the RJ and DJ distributions overlap significantly, and the method is fundamentally insensitive to PJ spectral structure [5]. Time-domain approaches, exemplified by the dual-threshold crossing (TLC) technique, infer jitter components from edge-crossing statistics and can accommodate the pattern dependence of ISI; however, when multiple PJ tones of comparable amplitude coexist, the selection of crossing levels becomes ambiguous, rendering the method ill-conditioned. Frequency-domain analysis directly reveals PJ spectral spurs and has been widely adopted in oscilloscope-based measurement instruments; nevertheless, constrained by the physical mechanism of the Fourier transform, conventional FFT-based methods produce severe spectral leakage when the observation window is finite or PJ frequencies are misaligned with the discrete sampling grid, causing substantial degradation in both frequency resolution and amplitude accuracy. Fig. 1 compares the FFT spectra under frequency-aligned and off-grid conditions: when the PJ frequency coincides exactly with a DFT grid point (Fig. 1a), spectral energy concentrates at a single bin and the amplitude is accurately recovered; when the frequency deviates from the grid (Fig. 1b, $f = 1.37$ MHz), the main-lobe energy leaks extensively into adjacent bins, resulting in severe amplitude underestimation—this constitutes the physical root cause of the systematic error exceeding 14 ps observed with LS-FFT in blind test scenarios.

To overcome this bottleneck, sparse recovery methods based on $L_1$ norm minimisation—represented by the LASSO algorithm—have emerged as a promising alternative by exploiting the inherent spectral sparsity of PJ sources [6]. However, in the blind test environments encountered by practical test instruments, conventional sparse methods still face two critical challenges: first, a heavy dependence on manual tuning of regularisation hyperparameters, lacking adaptability; second, the intrinsic nature of $L_1$ penalisation introduces systematic amplitude shrinkage bias, causing reconstructed PJ amplitudes to deviate significantly from their true physical values.

In recent years, deep learning-based jitter decomposition methods have also attracted attention. Wu et al. employed convolutional neural networks (CNN) for automatic classification and prediction of deterministic jitter components such as DCD, demonstrating the potential of data-driven approaches [7]. Peng et al. proposed a two-stage decomposition algorithm combining MAP gradient descent with MCMC refinement for the Dual-Dirac model, achieving accurate RJ/DJ separation under limited sampling conditions [8]. Nevertheless, these methods remain limited in super-resolution PJ frequency localisation and unbiased full-component amplitude reconstruction.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows: (1) FastRVM sequential sparse inference is introduced as a frequency pre-localisation mechanism into the jitter decomposition pipeline, compressing the effective dictionary size by 62% through a coarse full-band scan while maintaining frequency coverage, thereby alleviating the computational bottleneck of subsequent ARD inference. (2) The SBL-LS three-stage cascaded architecture is constructed, combining the adaptive frequency selection capability of ARD with the unbiased estimation property of ordinary least squares (OLS), enabling fully automated jitter decomposition without manual parameter tuning in blind test scenarios. (3) Through systematic wideband frequency sweep and multi-noise-level experiments, the parameter sensitivity of LASSO in blind test scenarios is quantitatively revealed, and the full-band accuracy advantage of the proposed method across the 100 kHz–15 MHz range is validated.

## 2. Physical Modelling and Sparse Representation of Jitter Signals

### 2.1 Time-Domain Observation Model of Jitter Components

According to the JEDEC standard [2], the time interval error (TIE) of the $k$-th transition edge is defined as:

$$y(k) = t_{act}(k) - t_{ref}(k) \tag{1}$$

Under the linear superposition assumption, the TIE sequence can be decomposed as:

$$y(k) = y_{RJ}(k) + y_{PJ}(k) + y_{ISI}(k) + y_{DCD}(k) + e(k) \tag{2}$$

where the individual terms correspond to random jitter, periodic jitter, inter-symbol interference, duty cycle distortion, and observation noise, respectively. Accordingly, this paper reformulates Eq. (2) as a linear regression problem within a redundant dictionary framework, exploiting the inherent spectral sparsity of PJ interference sources to achieve accurate decomposition.

### 2.2 Redundant Frequency Dictionary Architecture

The one-dimensional TIE sequence is reformulated as the following matrix-vector equation:

$$\mathbf{y} = [\mathbf{H}_{PJ} \quad \mathbf{H}_{ISI} \quad \mathbf{H}_{DCD}] \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_{PJ} \\ \mathbf{x}_{ISI} \\ \mathbf{x}_{DCD} \end{bmatrix} + \mathbf{v} \tag{3}$$

where $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1}$ denotes the TIE observation vector, $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$ is the global redundant dictionary containing numerous candidate frequencies, and $\mathbf{x}$ is the sparse coefficient vector to be estimated. Although the number of observations $N$ (typically 50,000) far exceeds the number of dictionary columns $M$ (approximately 1,633), making the system formally overdetermined, the number of candidate frequencies greatly exceeds the number of truly active PJ sources (usually only 1–3), and strong collinearity exists between adjacent columns on the frequency grid. Directly solving via ordinary least squares would forfeit frequency resolution due to energy leakage. Therefore, imposing sparsity constraints to precisely identify the few truly active frequencies from among a large number of highly correlated redundant candidate columns is the key mechanism for achieving high-resolution PJ decomposition. Fig. 2 illustrates the overall architecture of the global redundant dictionary $\mathbf{H}$: the left portion comprises the PJ sub-dictionary constructed from cosine–sine basis function pairs on a logarithmic–linear hybrid grid ($2K$ columns); the central portion is the $2^L$-column ISI pattern indicator matrix; and the right portion is a single-column DCD polarity vector. These three sub-dictionaries are concatenated to form the global design matrix with a total dimension of $M = 1{,}633$ columns.

The three sub-dictionaries are constructed as follows to address different physical mechanisms.

#### 2.2.1 Hybrid Frequency Grid for Periodic Jitter (PJ)

To overcome the picket-fence effect of the DFT, this paper adopts a logarithmic–linear hybrid grid strategy: logarithmic spacing (100 points) in the low-frequency range from 100 kHz to 1 MHz, and linear spacing with a step size of 100 kHz in the high-frequency range from 1 MHz to 20 MHz. The lower frequency bound of 100 kHz is determined by the observation window constraint: 50,000 points at 2 Gbps correspond to a 25 μs observation window, yielding a minimum resolvable frequency of approximately 40 kHz; 100 kHz is adopted as a conservative lower bound for engineering purposes. To accommodate PJ interference of arbitrary phase, an orthogonal cosine–sine basis function pair is introduced for each candidate frequency $f_i$ ($i = 1, \dots, K$):

$$\mathbf{h}_{c,i} = [\cos(2\pi f_i t_1), \dots, \cos(2\pi f_i t_N)]^T \tag{4}$$

$$\mathbf{h}_{s,i} = [\sin(2\pi f_i t_1), \dots, \sin(2\pi f_i t_N)]^T \tag{5}$$

Any PJ component with arbitrary phase $\phi_i$ can be linearly represented as $a_i \mathbf{h}_{c,i} + b_i \mathbf{h}_{s,i}$, transforming the nonlinear phase estimation problem into a linear coefficient estimation problem.

#### 2.2.2 Data-Dependent Jitter (DDJ) Sub-dictionary

ISI originates from channel memory effects. For a 2 Gbps scenario with a band-limited channel having a cutoff frequency of 2 GHz, a memory depth of $L = 5$ is adopted. $\mathbf{H}_{ISI}$ is an $N \times 2^L$ binary indicator matrix, where the $(n, j)$-th element equals 1 when the bit-pattern history of the $n$-th edge matches the $j$-th pattern, and 0 otherwise. DCD depends solely on the current edge polarity; $\mathbf{H}_{DCD}$ is an $N \times 1$ vector: $+1$ for rising edges and $-1$ for falling edges, corresponding to $\text{Pk-Pk}_{DCD} = 2|x_{DCD}|$.

### 2.3 Physical Interpretability of the Sparsity Prior

In practical links, only a few frequency points correspond to truly active PJ interference sources [1], endowing the coefficient vector $\mathbf{x}$ with strong intrinsic sparsity. For each identified active frequency $f_i$, the PJ peak-to-peak value is computed from the cosine and sine coefficients as:

$$\text{Pk-Pk}_{PJ,i} = 2\sqrt{a_i^2 + b_i^2} \tag{6}$$

Under the experimental parameters adopted in this paper for the 2 Gbps scenario ($N = 50{,}000$, $K \approx 800$), the logarithmic–linear hybrid grid construction maintains relatively low mutual correlation between adjacent candidate frequency columns, thereby reducing inter-column collinearity from an engineering perspective and facilitating stable convergence of sparse inference. It should be noted that the convergence of SBL is grounded in the ARD framework and evidence maximisation inference [9, 10], and is independent of the mutual coherence conditions associated with $\ell_0$ minimisation theory; the low-coherence dictionary design, as a beneficial engineering practice, further reduces the uncertainty of sparse inference.

## 3. Cascaded Decomposition Algorithm Combining Adaptive Identification and Unbiased Reconstruction

### 3.1 Motivation for Introducing the Sparse Recovery Framework

As discussed in Section 2.2, although the linear model $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}$ in this paper is formally overdetermined, the strong collinearity between frequency-adjacent candidate columns in the global dictionary causes direct OLS to lose frequency resolving capability. To address this problem, this paper proposes the SBL-LS three-stage cascaded decomposition architecture: **Stage 1** (Section 3.2) employs FastRVM for full-band coarse frequency localisation, rapidly compressing the effective dictionary size from approximately 1,600 columns to approximately 600 columns; **Stage 2** (Section 3.3) leverages the ARD mechanism of SBL to achieve hyperparameter-free adaptive frequency locking on the compressed sub-dictionary, outputting the sparse support set; **Stage 3** (Section 3.4) extracts the active sub-dictionary based on the identified support set and eliminates the amplitude shrinkage bias introduced by the ARD prior through unconstrained least squares correction. The three-stage design provides complementary capabilities in global search, inference accuracy, and estimation unbiasedness. The complete execution pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 1.

### 3.2 Stage 1: FastRVM Coarse Frequency Localisation

The PJ sub-matrix of the global redundant dictionary contains approximately $M_{full} \approx 1{,}600$ columns (dual cosine–sine bases). Running ARD inference directly at this scale involves repeated inversion of an $M_{full} \times M_{full}$ posterior covariance matrix, incurring prohibitive computational cost. To address this, a FastRVM-based coarse localisation strategy grounded in fast marginal likelihood maximisation is introduced to perform lightweight sparse inference across the full band, rapidly identifying active frequency regions.

In the implementation, $n_{coarse} = 100$ candidate frequency points are uniformly distributed over the interval $f_{min} = 100$ kHz to $f_{max} = 15$ MHz. For each point, a cosine–sine basis function pair is constructed, forming the coarse-grained design matrix $\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{coarse} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 200}$. Sequential sparse Bayesian inference iteratively updates the hyperparameter $\alpha_i$ and the corresponding Sparsity–Quality metric for each basis function:

$$\theta_i = Q_i^2 - S_i \tag{7}$$

where $S_i$ and $Q_i$ denote the sparsity and quality statistics of the $i$-th basis function with respect to the data [10]. When $\theta_i > 0$, the basis function is included in the active set; when $\theta_i \leq 0$, it is pruned. Upon convergence, the active frequency points are ranked in descending order of their summed weight variances, and the top-$K$ ($K = 3$ in this paper) candidate centre frequencies $\hat{f}_1, \hat{f}_2, \dots, \hat{f}_K$ are extracted.

Within the $\pm \Delta f$ ($\Delta f = 200$ kHz) neighbourhood of each candidate centre frequency $\hat{f}_k$, $n_{fine} = 100$ fine-grained frequency points are uniformly sampled. After merging and deduplication, these form the compressed fine candidate set $\mathcal{F}_{fine}$, containing approximately $M_{fine} \approx 600$ columns (three frequency bands × 200 points × dual bases). This represents a reduction of approximately 62% compared to the global dictionary, corresponding to an approximately 95% reduction in the matrix inversion computational cost during the ARD inference stage.

### 3.3 Stage 2: Fine Sub-band SBL Inference Based on ARD

On the compressed candidate set $\mathcal{F}_{fine}$, the fine PJ sub-dictionary $\mathbf{H}_{PJ,fine} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M_{fine}}$ is constructed and concatenated with the DCD column and the full ISI sub-matrix to form the reduced global dictionary $\mathbf{H}_{fine}$. Full ARD Bayesian inference is then performed on this dictionary.

SBL assigns each coefficient element $x_i$ an independent zero-mean Gaussian prior $p(x_i|\alpha_i) = \mathcal{N}(0, \alpha_i^{-1})$, where the hyperparameter $\alpha_i$ governs the prior precision of the $i$-th coefficient [10]. Through the MacKay evidence maximisation framework (Type-II maximum likelihood estimation), all hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and the noise variance $\sigma^2$ are jointly inferred from the data. **The essence of the ARD mechanism** is that when a candidate frequency is weakly correlated with the observed data, its corresponding $\alpha_i$ increases monotonically towards infinity during iteration, and the coefficient is automatically pruned to zero, thereby achieving unsupervised sparse frequency selection. The noise variance update rule is [10]:

$$(\sigma^2)^{(new)} = \frac{\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{H}_{fine}\boldsymbol{\mu}\|_2^2}{N - \sum_{i=1}^{M_{fine}}\gamma_i} \tag{8}$$

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the posterior mean and $\gamma_i \in [0,1]$ quantifies the degree to which the $i$-th feature is "determined" by the observed data. This mechanism enables automatic calibration of the noise variance throughout the inference process without any external parameter intervention—a core advantage over LASSO in blind test scenarios. The regularisation weight $\lambda$ of LASSO must be set based on prior knowledge of the noise level or through time-consuming cross-validation, and the $L_1$ penalty introduces unavoidable amplitude shrinkage on truly nonzero coefficients [6].

It should be noted that the implementation in this paper employs the ARD regression module in scikit-learn, whose default weakly informative hyperprior ($\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 10^{-6}$) corresponds to a nearly non-informative, extremely flat Gamma distribution. The influence of this hyperprior on inference results differs fundamentally in both magnitude and nature from the regularisation weight $\lambda$ of LASSO. Accordingly, the term "hyperparameter-free" in this paper specifically refers to the absence of manual intervention in blind test scenarios.

### 3.4 Stage 3: Physically Unbiased Reconstruction via Least Squares

#### 3.4.1 Analysis of Amplitude Shrinkage Bias

The posterior mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ from SBL is continuously constrained by the prior precision parameters $\alpha_i$, which is structurally equivalent to imposing an anisotropic $L_2$ regularisation penalty. This causes systematic underestimation of truly nonzero coefficients ($\mathbb{E}[\mu_i] < x_{\text{true},i}$). In jitter budget analysis for high-speed links, such underestimation directly leads to overly optimistic design margins, introducing the risk of system bit errors. Therefore, SBL must be positioned as an "intelligent frequency selector" rather than the final "amplitude estimator"; accurate amplitude extraction is accomplished by the LS correction in this stage.

#### 3.4.2 Extraction of the Active Frequency Sub-dictionary

After SBL convergence, the PJ weights are screened using a hard threshold $\epsilon = 10^{-20}$ to extract the effective support set:

$$\mathcal{S}_{PJ} = \{i : |\mu_{PJ, i}| > \epsilon\} \tag{9}$$

The set $\mathcal{S}_{PJ}$ is concatenated with the DCD index and the full ISI indices to form the global active index set $\mathcal{S}$. The sub-matrix is then extracted from the global dictionary by slicing:

$$\mathbf{H}_{sub} = \mathbf{H}[:, \mathcal{S}] \tag{10}$$

Since the number of true interference sources is very small, $M_{sub} \ll N$ after dimensionality reduction, and the inter-column collinearity is substantially diminished, transforming the system from an ill-conditioned overdetermined problem into a well-conditioned overdetermined one.

#### 3.4.3 Unconstrained Least Squares Correction and Physical Quantity Extraction

For the overdetermined system $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{H}_{sub}\mathbf{x}_{sub} + \mathbf{v}$, unconstrained ordinary least squares (OLS) is employed to recalculate the coefficient vector:

$$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{sub} = (\mathbf{H}_{sub}^T \mathbf{H}_{sub})^{-1} \mathbf{H}_{sub}^T \mathbf{y} \tag{11}$$

Since all prior penalty terms have been removed, and provided that the support set $\mathcal{S}$ coincides with the true active set, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{sub}$ constitutes the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) by the Gauss–Markov theorem. It should be noted that this unbiasedness is contingent upon the ARD stage correctly identifying the true active frequencies—if the support set contains omissions or false inclusions, the LS-stage estimates will be biased due to model misspecification. The experimental results in Section 4 demonstrate that, under the test conditions of this paper, the support set identification accuracy of ARD is sufficient to ensure the effectiveness of the LS correction. The physical quantities of each jitter component are extracted as follows: (1) **PJ peak-to-peak value**—computed from the cosine and sine basis coefficients as $\text{Pk-Pk}_{PJ,i} = 2\sqrt{\hat{a}_i^2 + \hat{b}_i^2}$; (2) **ISI/DCD**—obtained from the range or direct readout of the corresponding coefficients; (3) **RJ root mean square**—estimated from the sample standard deviation of the residual vector $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{H}_{sub}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{sub}$:

$$\hat{\sigma}_{RJ} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N-M_{sub}} \sum_{k=1}^{N} (\hat{v}_k - \bar{\hat{v}})^2 } \tag{12}$$

Compared with directly reading the prior noise variance $\alpha^{-1}$ from SBL, this residual-based empirical estimate exhibits greater physical reliability in practical oscilloscope measurement environments [11].

## 4. Experimental Validation and Results Analysis

### 4.1 Experimental Setup and Synthetic Dataset Construction

A high-speed serial signal jitter simulation platform was developed in Python. Synthetic data are employed such that the ground truth of each component is exactly known, providing a quantifiable benchmark for algorithm accuracy evaluation. The baseline simulation parameters are as follows: data rate 2 Gbps, oversampling rate 80 GS/s, 300,000-bit PRBS sequence per trial, with TIE sequences generated by extracting edge arrival times via zero-crossing linear interpolation. The four injected jitter components are: PJ (sinusoidal waveform, Pk-Pk fixed at 15.0 ps, frequency varied across experiments), ISI (fourth-order Bessel low-pass filter with cutoff frequency 2 GHz and memory depth $L = 5$; ISI Pk-Pk = 0.160 ps, derived from the range of zero-crossing deviations across all 32 five-bit patterns of the filtered waveform), DCD (fixed amplitude of 1.0 ps), and RJ (zero-mean Gaussian white noise, default RMS of 2.0 ps).

**Table 1.** Performance Comparison of Mainstream Frequency-Domain Jitter Decomposition Algorithms

| Algorithm | Hyperparameter Selection | Amplitude Estimation Bias | Frequency Resolution | Computational Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LS-FFT | None | Severely biased (spectral leakage) | Low | $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ |
| LASSO ($L_1$) | Manual / cross-validation | Systematically biased (shrinkage) | High | $\mathcal{O}(I_{iter}MN)$ |
| SBL-LS (Proposed) | **Automatic (ARD)** | **Low bias (LS stage)** | **High** | $\mathcal{O}(I_{iter}M^3)$ |

The baseline algorithms include LS-FFT (FFT peak detection followed by LS amplitude fitting) and LASSO ($L_1$ regularisation). The qualitative differences among the three algorithm classes in hyperparameter selection, amplitude estimation bias, and frequency resolution are summarised in Table 1, which also motivates the choice of these baselines. It should be noted that although five-fold cross-validation (5-fold CV) is the standard approach for LASSO hyperparameter optimisation, under the dictionary scale of this paper ($M = 1{,}633$ columns), each CV iteration requires solving the full redundant dictionary system, incurring computational costs exceeding five times that of a single LASSO run. This lacks engineering feasibility in high-speed instrument blind test scenarios [6] and essentially relies on prior knowledge of the noise level. Therefore, three representative parameter values $\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.10, 0.50\}$—corresponding to the under-regularised, empirically tuned, and over-regularised regimes commonly encountered in engineering practice—are adopted as the LASSO baselines. The objective is to quantitatively reveal the extreme sensitivity of LASSO performance to parameter selection, thereby highlighting the principal engineering advantage of the proposed SBL-LS algorithm, which achieves one-shot adaptive inference without manual parameter tuning across all experiments.

It should be noted that the synthetic PJ component in this paper adopts a sinusoidal waveform, consistent with the cosine–sine basis function assumption of the dictionary, constituting in-model validation conditions. To assess the impact of model mismatch, supplementary tests were conducted with triangular and square-wave PJ waveforms: for the triangular wave, whose fundamental component dominates the energy, SBL-LS can still capture the fundamental amplitude (relative error approximately 18%); for the square wave, whose energy is distributed across odd harmonics, the estimation error increases substantially (relative error 30%–88%) as this exceeds the representational capacity of the single-frequency sinusoidal dictionary. These results indicate that the proposed method is applicable when PJ sources exhibit spectral structures that can be approximated as discrete sinusoidal tones—an assumption that generally holds for common PJ sources in high-speed links such as power supply coupling jitter and clock crosstalk [1]. For non-sinusoidal periodic interference with significant harmonic content, a harmonic-extended dictionary is required to broaden the applicability. Future work will further validate the algorithm's engineering applicability using measured TIE data from high-speed serial links.

The proposed SBL-LS incorporates the FastRVM frequency pre-localisation strategy described in Section 3.2, compressing the effective dictionary size for ARD inference from $M_{full} \approx 1{,}600$ columns to $M_{fine} \approx 600$ columns. All SBL-LS results in the following experiments are based on this three-stage implementation (FastRVM coarse localisation → fine ARD inference → LS amplitude correction).

### 4.2 Wideband Periodic Jitter Estimation Accuracy Analysis

#### 4.2.1 Wideband PJ Amplitude Estimation Accuracy

In this experiment, the single-tone PJ Pk-Pk is fixed at 15.0 ps with a frequency sweep across the 100 kHz to 15 MHz wideband range, under a background RJ of 2.0 ps RMS. Fig. 2 presents the PJ amplitude estimation absolute error for each algorithm.

LS-FFT (grey dashed line) exhibits a constant error of 14.3–14.7 ps across the entire band, attributable to spectral leakage under non-aligned sampling that disperses the main-lobe energy into adjacent frequency bins, causing systematic amplitude underestimation. The three LASSO curves ($\alpha = 0.01/0.10/0.50$) reveal the parameter sensitivity dilemma in blind test environments: $\alpha = 0.10$ achieves an error of only 0.039 ps at 100 kHz but degrades to over 1.5 ps at higher frequencies; $\alpha = 0.50$ yields errors of 10.62 ps and 12.14 ps at 5 MHz and 15 MHz, respectively; $\alpha = 0.01$ exhibits an unstable profile with lower errors at mid-frequencies and elevated errors at both ends. The stark divergence among the three curves demonstrates that LASSO performance fundamentally depends on prior knowledge of the test scenario and lacks engineering repeatability in blind test conditions.

SBL-LS (Proposed) (red star-marked line), operating with no parameter intervention whatsoever, achieves errors within 0.24 ps across all six test frequencies (minimum 0.121 ps), substantially lower than the systematic error of LS-FFT. The advantage is particularly pronounced in the high-frequency band compared to over-regularised LASSO, supporting the effectiveness of the three-stage strategy in ensuring full-band accuracy.

#### 4.2.2 Multi-Component Joint Decomposition Capability Verification

Evaluating only the single PJ component error is insufficient to comprehensively reflect an algorithm's decoupling capability in complex links. To verify the algorithm's concurrent handling of multiple physical interference mechanisms, this section executes 50 independent Monte Carlo runs under the $f_{PJ} = 100$ kHz test condition (using 50,000 points, 40 μs observation window), with PRBS sequences and RJ noise realisations re-sampled for each run. The joint extraction performance across all four jitter components is presented in Table 2 and Fig. 3. Fig. 3 provides a bar-chart comparison of the estimated means and standard deviations for each algorithm across the PJ, DCD, ISI, and RJ dimensions (dashed lines indicate ground truth), clearly showing that all LASSO parameter settings produce zero ISI estimates, whereas SBL-LS is the only algorithm yielding nonzero responses for all four component types.

In the PJ dimension, SBL-LS achieves a mean error of only 0.063 ps (standard deviation 0.046), substantially outperforming LASSO ($\alpha = 0.01$) at 0.300 ps (standard deviation 0.059), with lower variability. Although LASSO ($\alpha = 0.10$) exhibits a low PJ mean error of 0.045 ps, this parameter represents a post-hoc optimised setting, and its DCD mean of 0.799 ps deviates approximately 20% from the ground truth. In the DCD and RJ dimensions, SBL-LS achieves 0.969 ± 0.020 ps and 1.999 ± 0.007 ps, respectively, closely matching the ground truth. In the ISI dimension, LASSO produces zero ISI estimates across all three parameter settings and all 50 runs (standard deviation 0.000). The root cause lies in the extremely small physical magnitude of the mean ISI pattern coefficients (approximately 0.01 ps per pattern), which fall well below the effective soft threshold imposed by the $L_1$ penalty at all three $\alpha$ settings, causing all ISI subspace coefficients to be shrunk to zero during the LASSO optimisation. This is fundamentally a regularisation threshold effect rather than structural incompatibility between the ISI and PJ sub-dictionaries; at $\alpha = 0.50$, the DCD estimate similarly degrades to 0.008 ± 0.011 ps (ground truth 1.000 ps), indicating that the suppressive effect of over-regularisation on weak components extends to the DCD subspace. SBL-LS is the only method among those compared that produces nonzero ISI estimates (0.347 ± 0.040 ps); the low standard deviation across 50 runs demonstrates statistical stability of the ARD mechanism in sensing weak ISI components. However, the estimated mean is approximately 2.2 times the ground truth (0.160 ps); this overestimation may originate from residual collinearity between the ISI pattern indicator matrix and low-frequency PJ candidate columns. Improving the estimation accuracy of weak ISI components constitutes an important direction for future work. Considering both mean accuracy and variance, SBL-LS exhibits the best overall multi-component joint decomposition performance and statistical repeatability under hyperparameter-free conditions.

### 4.3 Robustness Evaluation under Complex Noise Conditions

The experiment fixes the PJ frequency at 1 MHz with Pk-Pk = 15.0 ps, and increases $\sigma_{RJ}$ progressively from 0.5 ps to 5.0 ps, with a uniform training data length of 50,000 samples. Fig. 3 records the complete trajectory of PJ estimation error versus noise level for each algorithm.

The three LASSO parameter settings exhibit markedly different behaviour across the noise dimension. $\alpha = 0.10$ maintains an error of approximately 0.37–0.40 ps across the entire noise range, appearing superficially stable; however, this parameter was optimised post hoc for the 1 MHz scenario and fails when the test frequency changes (see Section 4.2, high-frequency band). $\alpha = 0.50$ shows a nearly constant error of 1.97–2.00 ps; this apparent stability is in fact a constant systematic error caused by $L_1$ amplitude shrinkage bias—even at the extremely low noise level of $\sigma_{RJ} = 0.5$ ps, the error remains as high as 2.0 ps, indicating that this parameter falls within the over-regularised regime. $\alpha = 0.01$ performs well in the low-noise region (error 0.020–0.037 ps) but degrades to 2.723 ps at $\sigma_{RJ} = 5.0$ ps, exposing the vulnerability of under-regularisation at high noise levels. Under the three parameter settings tested in this paper, LASSO fails to achieve stable performance across scenarios.

SBL-LS leads with the lowest error across the entire noise range (0.5–5.0 ps): the error is as low as 0.029 ps at the low-noise end ($\sigma_{RJ} = 0.5$ ps), increases approximately linearly with noise, and rises to 0.595 ps at $\sigma_{RJ} = 5.0$ ps. There is no apparent stability arising from coincidental matching between a fixed parameter and a specific scenario. The ARD mechanism autonomously estimates the noise variance $\sigma^2$ through Eq. (8) during iteration, achieving genuine adaptability to noise levels. Furthermore, SBL-LS maintains nonzero responses for weak components such as ISI and DCD within the same inference framework (see Table 2), whereas all LASSO variants produce zero ISI estimates, indicating that the systematic suppressive effect of $L_1$ penalisation on weak components persists under varying noise conditions.

It should be noted that the experiments in this section fix the PJ frequency at 1 MHz, and the conclusions are directly applicable to this frequency condition. In conjunction with the frequency sweep results of Section 4.2, it can be reasonably inferred that the noise robustness advantage of SBL-LS also holds at other frequency points, although specific error trajectories may differ. A two-dimensional joint sweep across all frequencies and noise levels will be a key focus of future validation work.

### 4.4 Ablation Study

To verify the independent contribution of each stage in the SBL-LS three-stage cascaded architecture, two ablation variants are designed: (1) removing Stage 1 FastRVM pre-localisation and running ARD inference and LS correction directly on the global dictionary ($M_{full} \approx 1{,}600$ columns), denoted as "w/o Stage 1"; (2) removing Stage 3 LS correction and using the ARD posterior mean directly as the final estimate, denoted as "w/o Stage 3". The estimation errors of the two variants and the complete SBL-LS across all four component types in the 100 kHz–15 MHz band are compared.

After removing FastRVM pre-localisation (w/o Stage 1), PJ errors at multiple frequency points deteriorate significantly to the 1–2 ps range, ISI errors climb to 2.5 ps in the high-frequency band, and RJ errors also degrade to above 3 ps. This indicates that the high dimensionality and strong inter-column collinearity of the global dictionary severely impair the convergence quality of ARD inference; FastRVM provides essential conditioning for subsequent fine inference by compressing the effective dictionary size by 62%. After removing LS correction (w/o Stage 3), PJ errors increase by approximately 0.2–0.4 ps compared to the complete SBL-LS, and DCD errors also increase slightly, confirming that the continuous constraint of the ARD prior precision parameters on nonzero coefficients indeed introduces appreciable amplitude shrinkage bias, and that the unconstrained recalculation in the LS stage is indispensable for eliminating this bias. The complete SBL-LS achieves the lowest or near-lowest errors across all frequency points and component types, quantitatively validating the complementarity of the three-stage design.

### 4.5 Computational Cost

As described in Section 3.2, FastRVM pre-localisation compresses the effective dictionary column count by 62%, and the resulting computational gain is further quantified in this section. The overall computational cost of the SBL-LS three-stage architecture is the superposition of the complexity of each stage. The FastRVM coarse localisation stage performs sequential sparse inference on only $n_{coarse} = 100$ coarse-grained candidate frequencies, with complexity $\mathcal{O}(I_{rvm} \cdot n_{coarse}^2)$, incurring negligible computational cost. The bottleneck of the fine ARD inference stage lies in the repeated inversion of the $M_{fine} \times M_{fine}$ posterior covariance matrix, with complexity $\mathcal{O}(I_{iter} M_{fine}^3)$; through FastRVM pre-localisation, the matrix inversion cost at the ARD stage is reduced by approximately 95% (see Section 3.2), substantially alleviating the primary computational bottleneck. The LS correction stage only requires solving an overdetermined system with $M_{sub} \ll N$, incurring negligible cost.

At the typical operating scale ($N = 50{,}000$), the complete SBL-LS analysis time with FastRVM is substantially reduced compared to running global ARD inference directly, but remains higher than LS-FFT (seconds) and LASSO (seconds, though the additional parameter search cost under blind test conditions must be considered). This gap is the inherent cost of achieving hyperparameter-free adaptive inference within the redundant dictionary framework. For the sub-picosecond jitter budget accuracy requirements in high-speed link signal integrity verification, the engineering value of high-accuracy unbiased reconstruction takes precedence over execution speed [3]. Accordingly, the target deployment scenarios for SBL-LS are offline deep analysis in high-end test instruments and precision ATE calibration, rather than real-time online monitoring. Future work will explore GPU-based parallel matrix computations to further reduce computational cost and extend the algorithm's applicability to semi-online scenarios [12].

## 5. Conclusion

This paper proposes a high-speed link jitter decomposition algorithm based on the SBL-LS three-stage cascaded architecture. FastRVM frequency pre-localisation compresses the effective dictionary size from approximately 1,600 columns to approximately 600 columns; the ARD mechanism of SBL subsequently achieves hyperparameter-free adaptive frequency locking on the compressed sub-dictionary; and the third-stage OLS correction eliminates the amplitude shrinkage bias introduced by the sparsity prior. In experiments on a 2 Gbps synthetic link, the proposed method achieves PJ estimation errors within 0.24 ps (minimum 0.121 ps) across the engineering-relevant 100 kHz–15 MHz band, substantially outperforming the 14.3–14.7 ps systematic error of LS-FFT. As the only method among those compared that simultaneously produces nonzero estimates for all four jitter components, SBL-LS achieves high-accuracy recovery of PJ and RJ under the condition of correct support set identification. It should be noted that the ISI estimate (0.346 ps) is approximately 2.2 times the ground truth (0.160 ps); this overestimation originates from residual collinearity between low-frequency PJ candidate columns and the ISI pattern indicator matrix, and improving the estimation accuracy of weak ISI components constitutes an important direction for future work. Constrained by the $\mathcal{O}(M_{\mathrm{fine}}^3)$ computational complexity, the current implementation is suited for offline analysis in high-end instruments and ATE calibration scenarios. Future work will advance along three directions: first, exploring GPU-based parallel matrix computations to reduce computational cost and extend applicability to semi-online scenarios; second, incorporating dictionary orthogonalisation strategies to improve weak ISI component estimation and low-frequency-band accuracy, further enhancing overall multi-component decoupling performance; and third, validating the algorithm's engineering applicability using measured TIE data from high-speed serial links under realistic non-ideal channel conditions.
